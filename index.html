Finding the Moon and Mars on Earth before we launch.

TERRA-TWIN screens all of Mongolia on a 1 km grid against lunar south-pole and Martian reference conditions, then scores the strongest candidates on four separate fidelity axes. The result is a ranked network of seven under-studied sites, from Holocene lava fields to buried glacier ice, each matched to an Artemis or Mars exploration target.

Why the current analog map falls short

The gap

Most analog fieldwork returns to the same handful of places: the Atacama, Hawaiʻi, Iceland, Antarctica's Dry Valleys. Each is excellent at one or two extremes, and each was chosen largely through tradition and access. Permanent bases need more than one extreme at once: stable lava-tube roofs, cold-trapped ice, traversable dust, and long-lived buried ice, all within reach of a supported field campaign.

Central Asia is almost absent from that map. Mongolia combines extreme continental cold (paper-reported surface minima down to 215 K), aridity, Holocene basalt, continuous permafrost, debris-covered glaciers and a confirmed impact crater inside one country with open skies and growing space ambitions.

Our approach

We replace hand-picked sites with a two-stage, open-data screening pipeline. Stage 1 grids the whole country at 1 km and measures each cell's distance from lunar and Martian reference conditions. Stage 2 scores the surviving hotspots with the Analog Fidelity Index (AFIX).

AFIX keeps fidelity decomposed into separate physical axes instead of one opaque "Mars-likeness" score, then discounts it honestly for how strong the evidence is and how reachable the site is.

03 // Technical pipeline

How we scored the country

A two-stage geospatial screening pipeline, designed to avoid selection bias: let the data nominate the sites, then test them hard.

Stage 1 · Macro screening. Mongolia (41.5–52.0°N, 87.7–119.9°E) is gridded at 1 km. Every cell gets a seven-element feature vector built from open Earth-observation archives: night-time minimum temperature, diurnal temperature range, vegetation index, annual precipitation, thermal-infrared emissivity, permafrost probability and SAR backscatter.

Screening criteria: aridity (<150 mm/yr), cold extremes, and mafic (basaltic) emissivity signatures.

# per-cell feature vector
x = [ Tnight,min, ΔTdiurnal, NDVI, P, εTIR, Ppermafrost, σ⁰SAR ]ᵀ
ERA5 / CHIRPS → precipitation, temperature
ASTER GED · EMIT → emissivity, mineralogy
MODIS LST · ESA Permafrost CCI → thermal, ground ice

Each feature is converted to a z-score and clipped at the 2nd and 98th percentiles so that a few extreme pixels cannot dominate.

Features where a lower value means higher off-world fidelity are inverted, so that "bigger" always means "more Moon- or Mars-like". Vegetation index and annual precipitation are the main examples: bare, dry ground scores high.

# standardise, clip, orient
zi = clip( (xi − μi) / σi , p2, p98 )
zNDVI ← −zNDVI
zP ← −zP

Each cell's normalised vector is compared with an off-world target reference vector built from NASA LRO Diviner lunar polar data and Mars SWIM ice and Jezero Crater data. Cells closest to a target form hotspot clusters (top-N percentile filter).

A feasibility filter then removes what a field team could not use: extreme terrain ruggedness, protected status and poor road logistics. Seven candidate regions survive.

# distance to Moon / Mars target
dk = ‖ z(x) − z(x*k) ‖ , k ∈ {Moon, Mars}
# keep low-d cells, then
hotspots = top-N(−dk) ∩ access ∩ ¬protected

Stage 2 · High-resolution fidelity. Sentinel-1/2, RADARSAT and LOLA / HiRISE target vectors feed four sub-scores. They are combined with normalised weights into AFIX.

AFIX is then adjusted by an evidence confidence factor E (how well verified the site's data are) and an operational accessibility factor A, giving AFIXadj on a 0–100 scale. A brilliant but unverified, hard-to-reach site cannot outrank a solid, reachable one.

# Analog Fidelity Index
AFIX = Σ wi · Si , i ∈ {therm, geol, rheo, morph} , Σ wi = 1
# operational adjustment
AFIXadj = AFIX × E × A

06 // Conclusion

One transect, two worlds

The screening supports a single integrated campaign instead of isolated tests: a north–south Khangai–Gobi Transect along the ~100°E meridian, linking Khorgo (48.18°N), the top dual Moon/Mars site, with Böön Tsagaan Nuur in the Valley of the Lakes (45.32°N), the top Mars-specific site, about 320 km apart in a straight line.

PHASE 1
Khorgo Volcanic ComplexRehearse lava-tube cavern mapping, surface radar backscatter calibration, and cold-region power management.
PHASE 2
Valley of the LakesTest autonomous sampling rovers on alkaline lake sediments and analyse carbonate deposits as analogs for Martian paleolakes.
PHASE II
Roadmap: validate the scoresDrone photogrammetry and field sensor networks at each site to ground-truth the orbital data, raise evidence factors, and re-run AFIX.
